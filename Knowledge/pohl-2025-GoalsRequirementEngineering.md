4.1 Establishing Vision in Context

Each requirement engineering process starts with an idea, the need or wish to change the current reality. Regardless of the complexity of the envisioned change, the essence of the desired change should be defined briefly and precisely. We call the definition of the envisioned change the system “vision”.

A vision may also express a small change to the current reality, such as the integration of a new functionality into a multimedia system, or the increase in the level of security of an online banking system. A vision only defines what should be changed, not how the change should be realised. In other words, the vision defines a mission but does not state how to achieve this mission. In the example of John F. Kennedy, the vision defines that a man has to be sent to the Moon and that he has to be brought back safely. It does not say anything, for example, about the transportation to be used, how to land on the Moon, or how to bring the man back to Earth again.

A vision should not express an unachievable illusion. Rather, it should describe a clearly defined and verifiable goal. The vision often defines a particular point in time when it should be achieved. The vision serves as guidance throughout the entire development process. The stakeholders should thus align their requirements and system development activities with the defined vision. 

Each software system is developed for a specific context (or set of contexts). Depending on the context, the realisation of the vision differs greatly. Consider, for example, the vision of a braking system stating that a vehicle should stop from a speed of 50 km/h within a distance of 10 meters. Depending on the context (including the kind of vehicle) the resulting system differs greatly. For example, the braking system of a tram is significantly different compared to the braking system of a motorbike. In some contexts, this vision can even hardly be realised at all – for example for a freight train or a cargo ship stopping within 10 meters from 50 km/h is close to impossible. We elaborate on the impact of the context and its influence on requirements engineering and system development in Part II. 

The vision and the system context are thus essential for requirements engineering and system development. An adequate consideration of the context is crucial for the success of the requirements engineering and system development process. While the vision is typically clearly defined, the system context is often not fully known and understood at the beginning of the requirements engineering process. The overall goal of requirements engineering is thus to “establish a vision within an existing context” (see, for example, Jarke and Pohl (1993), Pohl (1997)).

4.2 The Three Dimensions of Requirements Engineering

❑ Content dimension:  The content dimension deals with the understanding of the requirements and the context. At the beginning of the requirements engineering process, besides the system vision, only a few requirements and little information about the context are known. The understanding of the requirements and the context is thus typically vague. In contrast, at the end of the process, all the requirements and relevant context information should be known and understood. During the requirements engineering process, requirements and context information must thus be elicited. Moreover, new and innovative requirements must be developed. The key goal of requirements engineering derived from the content dimension is thus to ensure that “All relevant requirements and context information are explicitly known and understood at the required level of detail”. 

❑ Agreement dimension: The agreement dimension deals with the level of agreement achieved between the relevant stakeholders about the requirements and the context (information). At the beginning of a project, stakeholders typically have different opinions and conflicting views about the requirements and the context of the system. In contrast, at the end of the process, the stakeholders should have established a sufficient agreement about the requirements and the context. Conflicts between the stakeholders must be detected and resolved to achieve progress in the agreement dimension. If requirements are defined without consolidating thestakeholders’ different opinions, the unresolved conflicts will inevitably surface during later development stages, system deployment, or usage. The same is true for unresolved differing opinions about the context. Unresolved conflicts put the acceptance of the system at risk and thereby endanger the realisation of the system vision. The second key goal of requirements engineering is derived from the agreement dimension. This goal is thus to ensure that “A sufficient agreement about the requirements and the context is achieved between the relevant stakeholders”. 

❑ Documentation dimension: The documentation dimension deals with documenting and specifying relevant information (including requirements) using appropriate documentation and specification formats. This includes the creation of requirements artefacts, information about the context as well as other relevant information, such as intermediate work products. At the beginning of a project, documented information is typically non-compliant with documentation/specification guidelines. For example, requirements and context information are documented using simple notes, sketches, or hand drawings. Later in the process, the requirements and context information should be documented and specified according to the documentation and specification guidelines. Among other things, these guidelines define modelling languages or templates to be used for documenting requirements. The guidelines may also include criteria defining the required quality of the requirements artefacts, including consistency rules, as well as templates and rules for defining context information. The third goal of requirements engineering is thus to ensure that “All requirements and context information should be documented/specified in compliance with the relevant documentation/specification guidelines”. 

We thus define the three key goals of requirements engineering as follows:

Definition 4-1: Three Goals of Requirements Engineering 
Requirements engineering is a cooperative, iterative, and incremental process which aims at ensuring: 
(1) All relevant requirements and context information are explicitly known and understood at the required level of detail. 
(2) A sufficient agreement about the requirements and the context is achieved between the relevant stakeholders. 
(3) All relevant requirements and context information are documented and specified in compliance with the documentation/specification guidelines.

4.3 The Requirements Engineering Process Within the Three Dimensions

Although the three dimensions are orthogonal to each other, achieving progress in one dimension often impacts the progress achieved in the other two dimensions. For example, if additional requirements or context information are elicited (progress in the content dimension) the new requirements and context information could cause inconsistencies. Thus, the new requirements as well as the new context information can have a negative impact on the progress in the documentation dimension since documented requirements and context information should be consistent. Moreover, stakeholders might have different opinions about the new requirements or context information, which could cause additional conflicts. The new requirements and context information could thus also have a negative impact on the progress in the agreement dimension since those conflicts have to be resolved. In brief, the interdependencies between the three dimensions are:

❑ Content and agreement: A complete understanding of a requirement or context information does not imply sufficient agreement between the stakeholders about the requirement or context information. Similarly, agreement among stakeholders on a requirement or context information does not mean that the requirement or context information is understood fully and in sufficient detail. Progress in the agreement dimension (e.g., a creative solution to a conflict about requirements) can lead to new requirements or context information, which are not (yet) well understood, and thus to a step backwards in the content dimension. In turn, progress in the content dimension can lead to a step backwards in the agreement dimension. For example, eliciting new requirements or context information may lead to new conflicts between stakeholders or may uncover existing conflicts, or different understandings of requirements and context information. 
❑ Documentation and content: Documenting a requirement or context information according to the defined documentation guidelines does not imply a complete understanding of the requirement or context information. Vice versa, a complete understanding of a requirement or context information does not imply compliance of the documentation with the guidelines. Furthermore, progress in the documentation dimension (e.g., the formalisation of a requirement according to the guidelines) can lead to a step backwards in the content dimension. For example, documenting a requirement in accordance with the documentation guidelines may reveal missing information about the requirement. In turn, progress in the content dimension can lead to a step backwards in the documentation dimension. For example, the initial documentation of a newly elicited requirement does not immediately comply with the documentation guidelines. 
❑ Agreement and documentation: Agreement between stakeholders about a requirement or context information does not imply compliance of the documentation with the guidelines. Compliance of a documented requirement or context information with the documentation guidelines does not imply sufficient agreement of the stakeholders about the requirement or context information either. Progress in the documentation dimension can lead step backwards in the agreement dimension. Documenting a requirement or context information in accordance with the documentation guidelines can, for example, lead to a better understanding of the requirement or the context information and help stakeholders to recognise that they have a conflict with the requirement or context information. Similarly, progress in the agreement dimension can lead step backwards in the documentation dimension. For example, the stakeholders may resolve a conflict by agreeing on a new alternative requirement. The new requirement is recorded in the minutes, but it is not documented there in accordance with the documentation guidelines.

Nevertheless, to reach the three goals of requirements engineering progress is required in all three dimensions. This progress is achieved by executing core requirements engineering activities (derived from the three dimensions).
